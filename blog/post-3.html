What Actually Makes Technical Documentation Good

Documentation February 10, 2025 5 min read

I've spent a lot of time on the documentation side of customer support — writing runbooks, answering the same questions in ticket after ticket, and slowly realizing that most of those tickets existed because something wasn't documented well.

Start With Who You're Writing For

The most common mistake in technical writing isn't a grammar problem or a structure problem. It's an audience problem. When you write for everyone, you end up serving no one well.

Different personas need fundamentally different things. Technical decision-makers need high-level architectural overviews. End users need step-by-step getting-started guides. Developers need clear integration instructions. Mixing all three into one document creates content that confuses all of them.

There's a quote from one of the documentation leaders in the Mintlify guide that I keep coming back to: "You know how everything works, but that's detrimental to your end user." The knowledge gap between the person who built something and the person trying to use it is the root cause of most bad documentation. Bridging that gap starts with being honest about who you're actually writing for.

Use the Right Document Type for the Job

Once you know your audience, the next question is what kind of document they actually need. The guide introduces the Diátaxis framework — four content types, each designed for a different purpose:

Tutorials are for learning. They walk a beginner through a task step by step, prioritizing momentum over completeness. The goal is for the reader to finish with something working, not to understand everything at once.

How-to guides are task-focused. They assume the reader already knows what they want to do and just needs the steps — no hand-holding, no lengthy context-setting.

Reference docs are for experienced users who need quick, accurate information. Tables, consistent naming, copyable examples. Not the place for narrative or explanation.

Explanations answer "why" rather than "how." They provide background context and connect concepts for readers who want understanding, not instructions.

The discipline is picking one type per page before you start writing. A page that tries to be both a tutorial and a reference doc usually fails at both. As the guide puts it: "Don't be so inflexible in enforcing content types that you forget the reader" — but knowing which mode you're in before you write is what keeps things focused.

Consistency Matters More Than You Think

One insight from the style section that surprised me: consistency might matter more than any individual word choice. Switching between "API key" and "API token" throughout a guide creates real confusion — readers don't know if they're the same thing or different things, and they quietly stop trusting the document.

The guide is direct: "You may not be complimented on your consistency, but people will absolutely notice and be frustrated by a lack thereof."

A few other style principles that hold up in practice: active voice turns instructions into clear actions ("Click Save" beats "The save button can be clicked"). Second person ("you") keeps the reader in the frame. Short paragraphs and white space make content scannable — most users aren't reading linearly, they're hunting for one specific answer.

Navigation Is Your Subway Map

This analogy from the guide is the best I've heard: "Your navigation is like a subway map. It tells you how the whole system hangs together."

Good navigation does two things: it helps users find what they need, and it gives them a mental model of the whole product. Overcrowded top-level categories bury critical content. Unclear section labels make users guess. A flat wall of links is as disorienting as a subway map with no lines.

The practical test: bring in someone new — a new employee, a first-time user — and ask them to find something specific without helping them. Where they get stuck is where your navigation is broken. This is one of the cheapest and most effective doc-quality tests available.

Wrong Docs Are Worse Than No Docs

The last principle is the one I've seen violated the most. Documentation rots. Products change, steps get stale, screenshots become outdated. The temptation is to keep everything "just in case someone needs it" — but inaccurate documentation actively misleads users and erodes trust.

The Mintlify guide is blunt about this: "Wrong docs can be worse than no docs." Removing content that can't be kept accurate is a legitimate editorial decision, not a failure.

The better long-term approach: write for longevity. Use links instead of duplicating steps across pages. Keep time-sensitive information (announcements, workarounds, deprecation notes) out of core docs. Design each page so that when the product changes, there's one place to update — not five.

Final Thoughts

Good documentation doesn't require being a professional writer. It requires being honest about who your reader is, choosing the right format, staying consistent, and designing for the reality that things will change.

The companies with the best docs — Stripe, GitHub, Twilio — didn't get there because their engineers write beautifully. They got there because someone thought systematically about what readers need and built a process around maintaining that quality over time.
